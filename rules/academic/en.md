# Academic writing guide: English posts

The register of an international university website: scholarly but plain. Write the way a university communications office writes for students, staff, families and peer institutions. Precise, objective and measured. Never promotional, never chatty, never a research paper.

The style guide and "Learned from the owner's edits" win over this guide when they differ.

## Spelling and form
- British English throughout: organised, recognised, specialised, emphasised, utilised (better: used), honour, centre, colour, behaviour, defence, analyse, catalogue, programme (for an academic or event programme).
- Keep official names exactly as listed, even if their spelling differs.
- No contractions (do not, it is, they have). No exclamation marks, no question marks, no emojis.
- Numbers as the owner gave them. Dates and titles exactly as given.

## Voice
- Third person only. The institution is "the college", "the department", "the Deanship", "the university". Never "we", "our", "us", "you" or "your".
- Objective: state what happened and what it was designed to achieve. Do not judge it ("a great success", "an impressive turnout").
- Measured verbs: aims to, seeks to, contributes to, supports, forms part of, is intended to, reflects.
- Passive voice is acceptable for events ("The session was delivered by…", "Training was conducted in…"). Use the active voice when the actor matters.
- One idea per sentence. Most sentences 15 to 25 words. Vary length a little; never chain clauses with "and" more than once.
- Keep one tense per paragraph: past for completed events, present or future for announcements.

## Precise vocabulary
Prefer the discipline's own terms over general words:
- "clinical training", "laboratory session", "practical component", "curriculum", "learning outcomes", "accreditation", "quality assurance", "peer review", "continuing professional development", "community engagement".
- "students of the third stage" or "third-year students" as the owner wrote it; do not guess.
- Use "activity", "session", "workshop", "seminar", "lecture" for what it actually was; do not upgrade a lecture to a conference.

## Avoid (common in machine-written text)
- Hype and promotion: exceptional, outstanding, remarkable, world-class, cutting-edge, unparalleled, state-of-the-art, prestigious, vibrant, thriving.
- Informal words: amazing, awesome, fantastic, wonderful, huge, a lot of, lots of, stuff, kids, guys, super, fun.
- Machine clichés: delve, pivotal, tapestry, testament to, underscore, showcase, foster (overused), navigate the landscape, in today's fast-paced world, plays a vital role, a beacon of, embark on a journey.
- Empty openers and fillers: "It is worth noting that", "It is important to mention", "In conclusion", "Overall".
- Stacked hedges: "may potentially help to possibly improve". One qualifier at most, and only when the owner was unsure.
- Lists of three by habit ("knowledge, skills and values") when only one or two apply.
- Dashes used as decoration. Use a comma or a new sentence.
- Rhetorical questions, metaphors, slogans, appeals to the reader ("join us", "do not miss").

## Paragraph shape (news)
1. What took place, who organised it, who took part, as part of what course or plan.
2. How it was carried out and what participants gained, in concrete academic terms.
3. The institutional framing: the Deanship or unit's plan, quality standards, the university's mission (general wording, listed in the review notes).
4. The aim, stated plainly.

## Example of the register
Weak: "We were thrilled to host an amazing workshop that really showcased our students' incredible skills!"
Right: "The College of Nursing held a workshop on infection control for third-year students, as part of the practical component of the curriculum."
